Four problems, measured and solved

Each one the same way: time it first, change one thing, then time it again the same way.

10,000slimes in one horde

A horde of thousands, for a party of four

The problem. The Endless is a survival minigame where the crowd keeps growing. As ordinary monsters, each one an object with its own physics, a few hundred is already heavy, and I wanted thousands, on a phone, for four players at once.

What I did. A slime isn't an object at all: it's one row of numbers in a few packed arrays, moved 20 times a second and drawn all at once. Invisible stand-ins move onto the slimes nearest the fighting, so every weapon in the game still hits them. In a party, every game grows the same slimes from a shared seed, so no slime's position is ever sent over the network.

The result. The Testing Ground holds 10,000. A step of 3,000 takes under 7 ms on my test machine, and phones keep the crowd at 1,000.

504 → 52a false heal, trimmed by the server

A server that doesn't believe the game

The problem. Anyone can edit their own copy of a game. In an online game, that means every number the game reports could be a lie.

What I did. The server keeps everything worth having: level and XP, gold in a double-entry ledger, every loot roll, every bag. A kill pays only if the server's own count of that fight agrees, and a heal it can't explain is trimmed back to what regeneration could honestly give.

The result. In September a bug of my own healed characters on the player's machine. The server caught it every time, trimming 504 health back to 52 with a line in the log, until I read the log and fixed the game. There are 47 written security rules, and each names the test that proves it.

170 msfaster into the biggest area

The Big Field, faster to arrive and to leave

The problem. The Big Field is the largest map: 4,555 wall pieces, each a small scene of four or five objects, and a floor of 136,000 tiles that was being kept twice.

What I did. I timed arriving and leaving part by part before changing anything. The walls became plain pictures with one collision body per chunk of the map, taking 18,846 objects down to about 4,600, and the floor is now kept once, with the map's picture made ahead of time.

The result. About 170 ms faster to arrive and 150 ms faster to leave on my test machine, and nothing looks different: the floor and the map match the old ones to the pixel.

17 → 0.8 msper notch of the zoom

Zooming without the stutter

The problem. Zooming in and out felt heavy. Each notch of the mouse wheel took up to 17 ms in the Big Field, a stutter you could feel.

What I did. I measured it first: almost all of that time was the game searching all 16,700 objects in the map to find its one camera. Now it remembers each camera as it appears.

The result. 0.3 to 0.8 ms a notch, most of it saving the setting, and a test makes sure the search can't come back.

How I work

I build with AI as my hands. Claude writes most of the code; I decide what gets built, play every change, and don't accept anything that isn't measured. Every speed-up on this page has a before and an after, taken the same way. Every rule on the server names the test that proves it, and every new test is watched failing on purpose before it's trusted.

My notes are the project's memory: an engineering log of every lesson that cost an afternoon, written rules for how a change reaches my machine, and one script that ships every release the same way. That's the skill I'm proudest of: steering a big system with AI without letting it drift.
